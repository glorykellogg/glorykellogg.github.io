Butterfly Wing Pattern Formation

Studying how wing patterns form through observation and simulation

I study how butterfly wing patterns form and what their final appearance can tell us about how they developed. My project brings together observations of painted lady butterflies, image processing, and mathematical models.

I started this project independently in July 2026. Since October 2026, I have been working under the guidance of Dr. Megan Morrison at Illinois Tech.

Raising the Butterflies

On July 17, 2026, I received 10 painted lady caterpillars. Over the following weeks, I raised butterflies and documented their development through photographs and a diary. My observations continued through September.

The butterfly-raising stage is now complete. I still need to scan their wings and prepare the images for comparison with my simulations.

A Morphogen-Based Model

How could a chemical signal help determine where a butterfly’s wing becomes pigmented?

One proposed explanation involves a morphogen, a chemical signal that influences how cells develop. In Murray’s model, a morphogen spreads across the developing wing and activates a local response that affects later pigmentation. A temporary chemical signal can therefore leave a lasting pattern.1

The equations below describe the Murray-based model implemented in Edwin Nongba Traore’s 2017 report.2

A Signal Spreads and Breaks Down

Let \(S\) represent the morphogen concentration at a particular location and time. Its concentration changes according to

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = \underbrace{D\nabla^2 S}_{\text{diffusion}} - \underbrace{K\gamma S}_{\text{degradation}}. \]

The left side describes how quickly the concentration changes over time. On the right, diffusion spreads the signal, while degradation removes it.

The parameter \(D\) controls diffusion, \(K\) controls degradation, and \(\gamma\) is a size-scaling parameter in this model. The symbol \(\nabla^2\), called the Laplacian, describes the spatial variation in concentration used to calculate diffusion.

Together, these processes affect how far the signal travels and how long it remains present.

Cells Respond to the Signal

The second equation describes a gene product, a molecule made when a gene is expressed. In this model, the gene product affects later pigmentation.

Let \(g\) represent its concentration:

\[ \frac{\partial g}{\partial t} = \gamma \left( k_1 S + \frac{k_2 g^2}{1+g^2} - k_3 g \right). \]

Each term describes a different part of the response:

  • Activation by the signal: \(k_1 S\) increases gene-product production in response to the morphogen.
  • Self-activation: \(\frac{k_2 g^2}{1+g^2}\) allows existing gene product to encourage further production. This effect levels off as \(g\) increases.
  • Degradation: \(-k_3 g\) removes gene product over time.

For suitable parameter values, self-activation can keep the gene product at a positive level even after the original signal fades. This creates a switch: a cell can change its developmental state and remain in that state after the signal is gone.3

The model therefore separates two things: the temporary signal moving across the wing and the lasting response it leaves behind.

Wing Shape and Boundaries

The shape and size of the wing affect where signals can spread. Murray’s work explores how changes in geometry and scale can produce different patterns even when the underlying mechanism remains the same.4

The model described in Traore’s report treats wing edges and veins as barriers to diffusion. This is expressed through a zero-flux boundary condition:

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial \mathbf{n}}=0 \qquad \text{on } \partial\Omega. \]

Here, \(\Omega\) is the region being modeled, \(\partial\Omega\) is its boundary, and \(\mathbf{n}\) points perpendicular to that boundary. The condition means that no morphogen crosses the boundary.

This is an assumption of the model, not a claim that every real wing vein completely blocks biological signals.

How This Relates to Turing’s Work

Alan Turing’s work shows how chemical reactions and diffusion can amplify small differences in concentration into spatial patterns.5

The model above uses a different route: it starts with morphogen placed in particular regions, lets that signal spread, and uses a local gene switch to record its effects. It does not rely on a Turing instability to generate the initial pattern.

These ideas provide mathematical starting points for my project. I am investigating what they can explain, rather than assuming that one model captures every part of butterfly wing development.

Building the Model

I am developing a computational framework to simulate how chemical signals might spread, break down, and affect pattern formation across a developing wing.

To represent the wing’s shape, I use an adaptive Delaunay–Voronoi mesh. This divides the wing into small, connected regions where the signals can be simulated.

Working Backward from Patterns

The project focuses on an inverse problem: starting with a wing pattern and working backward to find model settings that could produce it.

I use CMA-ES, an optimization method, to search for model parameters and signal source locations that produce patterns similar to a target image. Once I have scanned the wings of the butterflies I raised, I will use those images as targets.

For now, I am testing the workflow with reference wing images from Ed Yong’s article, “Scientists Can Now Repaint Butterfly Wings”.

My main questions are:

  • Can a final wing pattern tell us where developmental signals began and how they spread?
  • Can different model parameters or processes produce similar patterns?
  • How does the shape of the wing affect both the simulated pattern and what we can learn from it?

These questions involve parameter identifiability: whether the available observations can distinguish one set of model parameters from another.

A close visual match is not enough to show that the model has recovered the actual developmental process. Different explanations may fit the same pattern.

Next Steps

  • Scan the butterfly wings and prepare the images for analysis.
  • Compare simulated patterns with the observed wing patterns.
  • Test how reliably model parameters and signal source locations can be recovered, and where different explanations remain possible.

The goal is not just to reproduce a wing pattern, but to understand what that pattern can tell us about its development and what remains uncertain.
