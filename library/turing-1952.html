The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis (1952)

Citation

Turing, A. M. (1952). The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London. Series B, Biological Sciences, 237(641), 37–72. https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1952.0012

Read the paper.

Overview

Turing proposes that biological patterns can emerge from a system of interacting chemical substances, which he calls morphogens. These substances react with one another and diffuse through tissue.

A spatially uniform state can become unstable under the right conditions. Small differences in morphogen concentration may then grow rather than disappear, producing nonuniform patterns.

Main Ideas

Morphogens

  • Morphogens are chemical substances involved in the development of biological form.
  • Cells do not follow a fixed developmental blueprint.
  • Instead, cells respond to local morphogen concentrations that change over time.

Reaction and Diffusion

The model combines two processes:

  • local chemical reactions;
  • diffusion across space.

A simplified reaction–diffusion system is

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = D_u \nabla^2 u + f(u,v), \]

\[ \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} = D_v \nabla^2 v + g(u,v). \]

where

  • \(u\) and \(v\) are morphogen concentrations.
  • \(D_u\) and \(D_v\) are diffusion coefficients.
  • \(f(u,v)\) and \(g(u,v)\) describe the local reaction terms.
  • \(\nabla^2\) is the Laplacian operator.

Diffusion-Driven Instability

  • A spatially uniform equilibrium can become unstable when diffusion is added.
  • Diffusion normally smooths differences in concentration.
  • In a coupled reaction–diffusion system, unequal diffusion rates can instead amplify small perturbations.
  • Those perturbations can develop into stable spatial patterns.

Pattern Formation

Possible outcomes include

  • spots;
  • stripes;
  • waves;
  • repeated structures;
  • other spatial arrangements.

The resulting pattern depends on

  • the reaction terms;
  • the diffusion coefficients;
  • the geometry of the domain;
  • the initial perturbations.

Questions

  • Why does diffusion stabilize some systems but destabilize others?
  • What determines whether spots or stripes emerge?
  • How does the geometry of the domain influence the final pattern?
  • How should a reaction–diffusion model be adapted to an irregular butterfly wing?
  • Can diffusion on a graph preserve meaningful geometric information?
  • Which aspects of butterfly wing development are well described by Turing models, and which require additional biological mechanisms?

Graph-Based Diffusion

A graph-based analogue replaces the continuous Laplacian with the graph Laplacian.

The system becomes

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt} = -D_uL\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}), \]

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = -D_vL\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}). \]

These equations combine two competing processes:

  • Graph diffusion (\(-D_uL\mathbf{u}\) and \(-D_vL\mathbf{v}\)), which spreads morphogens across the graph.
  • Local reactions (\(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\) and \(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\)), which describe chemical interactions at each node.

Terms

  • \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are vectors of morphogen concentrations, with one value assigned to each node of the graph.
  • \(\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}\) and \(\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}\) are the rates of change of those concentrations over time.
  • \(t\) is time.
  • \(D_u\) and \(D_v\) are diffusion coefficients.
  • \(L\) is the graph Laplacian, which describes how quantities diffuse between neighboring nodes.
  • \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\) and \(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\) describe the local reaction dynamics at each node.

Biological Interpretation

Rather than allowing morphogens to diffuse across a continuous surface,

  • diffusion occurs along the edges of a graph;
  • each node represents a location on the butterfly wing;
  • the graph structure determines the possible diffusion pathways.

This formulation naturally accommodates irregular geometries and may better capture wing veins and compartment boundaries than a regular finite-difference grid.

Notes

Homogeneous Equilibrium

  • Morphogen concentrations are constant throughout the domain.
  • Concentrations do not change over time.
  • The reaction system must be stable before diffusion is introduced.
  • Turing instability occurs when diffusion destabilizes this equilibrium.

Linear Stability

  • Small perturbations are analyzed by linearizing the reaction terms near an equilibrium.
  • The Jacobian matrix is

\[ J = \begin{pmatrix} f_u & f_v \\ g_u & g_v \end{pmatrix}. \]

  • The entries of \(J\) determine the local stability of the reaction system.
  • With diffusion, each spatial mode evolves independently.
  • Some modes decay while others grow into observable patterns.

Geometry

  • Geometry determines which spatial modes are possible.
  • Boundary conditions influence how morphogens behave near the edges of the domain.
  • Domain shape affects the resulting biological pattern.
  • Geometry is therefore a central component of models for butterfly wings, shells, and animal coat markings.

Vocabulary

  • Morphogen — A chemical signaling molecule whose concentration varies across tissue and provides positional information during development.

  • Homogeneous equilibrium — A steady state in which morphogen concentrations are the same everywhere in space and remain constant over time.

  • Perturbation — A small disturbance from an equilibrium state used to test whether the system returns to equilibrium or evolves into a new pattern.

  • Linear stability — A method of analyzing how small perturbations behave near an equilibrium by approximating the system with its linear terms.

  • Diffusion coefficient — A parameter that determines how quickly a morphogen spreads through space.

  • Laplacian — A differential operator, denoted \(\nabla^2\), that measures how a quantity diffuses by comparing its value to the surrounding region.

  • Eigenmode — A spatial pattern of variation that grows or decays independently during stability analysis. The dominant growing eigenmode determines the emerging biological pattern.

  • Boundary condition — A mathematical constraint imposed at the edges of the domain that influences how diffusion and reactions behave.

  • Diffusion-driven instability (Turing instability) — A phenomenon in which diffusion, rather than smoothing differences, destabilizes an otherwise stable homogeneous equilibrium and produces spatial patterns.

  • Graph Laplacian — The graph-theoretic analogue of the continuous Laplacian. It models diffusion on discrete structures such as networks or graph representations of butterfly wings.
