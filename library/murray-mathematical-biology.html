On Pattern Formation Mechanisms for Lepidopteran Wing Patterns and Mammalian Coat Markings (1981)

Journal Article

J. D. Murray

Citation

Murray, J. D. (1981). On Pattern Formation Mechanisms for Lepidopteran Wing Patterns and Mammalian Coat Markings. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London. Series B: Biological Sciences, 295(1078), 473–496. :contentReferenceoaicite:0

Status

Currently reading.

Why I’m Reading It

This paper is especially relevant to my butterfly wing project because it is one of the earliest mathematical attempts to explain wing pattern formation through diffusion and gene activation rather than simply describing the observed patterns.

It also serves as an important bridge between Turing’s abstract reaction–diffusion theory and biologically motivated models of butterfly development.

Overview

Murray proposes a mathematical mechanism capable of generating common butterfly wing patterns using a diffusing morphogen coupled with a threshold gene-activation process. He then compares the predictions of the model with experimental results from butterfly and moth wings.

The second half of the paper extends similar mathematical ideas to mammalian coat patterns, arguing that geometry and scale may explain much of the diversity observed in nature. :contentReferenceoaicite:1

Main Ideas

Pattern Formation Requires a Mechanism

Rather than asking what pattern exists, Murray asks

What biological process could generate that pattern?

His philosophy is that mathematical biology should explain mechanisms, not merely describe observations.

Diffusing Morphogens

The model assumes that a morphogen is released from specific locations on the wing.

As it diffuses,

  • its concentration decreases,
  • cells measure the local concentration,
  • genes switch on once a threshold is exceeded.

This produces permanent spatial structure from a temporary chemical signal. :contentReferenceoaicite:2

Gene Activation as a Switch

One particularly elegant aspect of the paper is the addition of a biochemical switch.

Rather than pigment changing continuously with morphogen concentration, cells permanently switch into a new developmental state once the morphogen exceeds a critical threshold.

That idea makes the resulting patterns stable even after the morphogen disappears. :contentReferenceoaicite:3

Geometry Matters

One of Murray’s central claims is that geometry is not merely a boundary condition—it actively shapes the pattern.

Changing

  • wing size,
  • wing shape,
  • vein arrangement,

can dramatically alter the resulting pigmentation while leaving the underlying equations unchanged. :contentReferenceoaicite:4 :contentReferenceoaicite:5

Connections to My Research

This paper overlaps with nearly every stage of my butterfly wing project.

  • graph representations of wing geometry
  • morphogen diffusion
  • mathematical models of development
  • image-based comparison between simulations and real butterflies
  • the influence of wing veins on pigmentation

One question I’m especially interested in is whether Murray’s continuous diffusion model can be reformulated on a graph using graph Laplacians.

Questions

  • How well has this model held up over the last forty years?
  • Which parts of the mechanism have experimental support today?
  • How would the model change if diffusion occurred on a graph instead of a continuous surface?
  • Could real wing vein networks be extracted automatically from photographs and used directly in simulations?
  • How do modern models incorporate gene regulatory networks in addition to morphogen diffusion?

Favorite Ideas

Temporary Signals Can Produce Permanent Patterns

A short-lived chemical signal can permanently determine cell identity through threshold dynamics.

That simple idea appears repeatedly throughout developmental biology.

Mathematics Explains Form

This paper is a great example of mathematics being used to propose a biological mechanism rather than simply fitting experimental data.
