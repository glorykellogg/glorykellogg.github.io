A Mathematical Model for Butterfly Wing Patterns

Citation

Traore, E. N. (2017). A Mathematical Model for Butterfly Wing Patterns. Project report supervised by Filippo Remonato and Mats Ehrnstrøm.

Overview

The report studies a mathematical model based on two interacting processes:

  1. A morphogen diffuses across the wing and degrades over time.
  2. The local morphogen concentration activates a gene product that controls later pigmentation.

The author implements the model numerically and investigates how changes in parameters, domain size, and initial morphogen placement affect the resulting wing pattern.

The Model

Morphogen Diffusion

The morphogen concentration \(S\) is governed by

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = D\nabla^2S - K\gamma S. \]

The equation combines two competing processes:

  • Diffusion (\(D\nabla^2S\)), which spreads the morphogen across the wing.
  • Degradation (\(-K\gamma S\)), which removes morphogen over time.

where

  • \(S\) is the morphogen concentration.
  • \(\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}\) is the rate of change of \(S\) over time.
  • \(D\) is the diffusion coefficient.
  • \(\nabla^2S\) measures how the concentration differs from its surroundings.
  • \(K\) controls the degradation rate.
  • \(\gamma\) is a scaling parameter related to wing size.

Gene-Product Activation

The gene product \(g\) satisfies

\[ \frac{\partial g}{\partial t} = \gamma \left( k_1S + \frac{k_2g^2}{1+g^2} - k_3g \right). \]

The equation combines three competing processes:

  • Activation by the morphogen (\(k_1S\)), which increases \(g\) when the local morphogen concentration is high.
  • Nonlinear self-activation \(\left(\frac{k_2g^2}{1+g^2}\right)\), in which existing gene product promotes the production of more gene product.
  • Degradation (\(-k_3g\)), which removes gene product over time.

where

  • \(g\) is the concentration of the gene product, the molecule that ultimately influences pigmentation.
  • \(\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}\) is the rate at which the gene-product concentration changes over time.
  • \(S\) is the local morphogen concentration.
  • \(k_1\) controls how strongly the morphogen activates gene production.
  • \(k_2\) controls the strength of self-activation.
  • \(k_3\) controls the degradation rate.
  • \(\gamma\) scales the overall speed of the system.

The nonlinear self-activation term

\[ \frac{k_2g^2}{1+g^2} \]

has three important properties:

  • Nonlinear means the rate of activation does not increase at a constant rate as \(g\) increases.
  • Self-activation means that gene product already present encourages the production of more of itself.
  • The denominator \(1+g^2\) prevents the activation from increasing indefinitely, causing the term to level off at high concentrations.

Together, self-activation and degradation create a biological switch:

  • Below a critical morphogen concentration, gene production eventually stops and \(g\) returns to zero.
  • Above the threshold, the gene product can stabilize at a positive value.
  • This stable “on” state corresponds to cells that later become pigmented.

Boundary Conditions

The model assumes that morphogen cannot diffuse across

  • the outer edge of the wing;
  • wing veins.

This is expressed by the zero-flux boundary condition

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial \mathbf{n}} = 0 \qquad \text{on } \partial\Omega. \]

where

  • \(S\) is the morphogen concentration.
  • \(\frac{\partial S}{\partial \mathbf{n}}\) is the rate at which the morphogen changes in the direction perpendicular to the boundary.
  • \(\mathbf{n}\) is the outward normal vector, a vector pointing directly away from the boundary.
  • \(\partial\Omega\) denotes the boundary of the computational domain.

Numerical Method

Finite Differences

  • The continuous wing is replaced by a finite grid.
  • Spatial derivatives are approximated using finite differences.
  • The Laplacian is computed with a second-order central-difference stencil.

Ghost Nodes

  • Ghost nodes enforce the zero-flux boundary condition.
  • Artificial boundary values are chosen so that the normal derivative is zero.
  • This prevents morphogen from leaving the computational domain.

Time Integration

  • Time evolution is computed using the forward Euler method.
  • The emphasis is on reproducing qualitative biological patterns rather than high numerical accuracy.

Main Results

Reproducing Cautery Experiments

The simulations reproduce Murray’s cauterization experiments.

  • Holes placed near the morphogen source interrupt pigment-band formation.
  • Holes farther from the source allow pigment bands to develop before diffusion is disrupted.

Effect of Scale

The parameter ()

  • changes wing size without changing the computational grid;
  • alters the relative size of the pigmented region.

As () increases, the activated region occupies a smaller portion of the wing.

Constructing a Wing-Shaped Domain

The computational wing is created by

  • selecting boundary points;
  • interpolating them with Lagrange polynomials;
  • embedding the resulting outline in a square computational grid.

Multiple Morphogens

The report also investigates multiple interacting morphogen systems.

  • Two morphogen fields are simulated simultaneously.
  • Different gene products produce separate pigment regions.
  • The approach suggests a pathway toward more complex wing patterns.

Strengths

  • Converts Murray’s theoretical model into a numerical simulation.
  • Clearly explains the governing equations.
  • Clearly explains the boundary conditions.
  • Clearly explains the numerical implementation.
  • Uses a wing-shaped computational domain rather than a simple rectangle.
  • Demonstrates how parameter changes alter biological patterns.
  • Bridges mathematical theory and computational experimentation.

Limitations

  • Treats the wing as a largely homogeneous domain.
  • Does not explicitly model many structural features of real butterfly wings.
  • Represents geometry indirectly through a finite-difference grid.
  • A graph-based or irregular-mesh representation may better preserve wing veins and compartment boundaries.

Questions

  • How sensitive are the simulated patterns to grid resolution?
  • Would a higher-order or implicit time-stepping method change the results?
  • Should diffusion coefficients vary across veins or wing compartments?
  • Could wing boundaries be extracted directly from photographs?
  • Could the graph Laplacian replace the finite-difference Laplacian?
  • How should multiple morphogens be coupled?
  • How can simulated gene-product fields be compared quantitatively with observed pigmentation?

Influence on My Work

This report makes the transition from theory to implementation concrete.

It demonstrates that Murray’s model can be simulated on an idealized butterfly wing using finite differences.

My project may instead

  • extract wing geometry directly from photographs;
  • represent the wing as a graph;
  • simulate diffusion using the graph Laplacian;
  • compare simulated fields with observed butterfly wing patterns.

A graph-based approach may provide

  • a more faithful representation of irregular geometry;
  • explicit treatment of veins and wing compartments;
  • a direct connection between measured wing structure and computation;
  • quantitative comparison between simulations and real butterfly wings.
