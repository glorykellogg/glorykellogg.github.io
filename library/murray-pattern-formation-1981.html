On Pattern Formation Mechanisms for Lepidopteran Wing Patterns and Mammalian Coat Markings (1981)

Citation

Murray, J. D. (1981). On Pattern Formation Mechanisms for Lepidopteran Wing Patterns and Mammalian Coat Markings. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London. Series B: Biological Sciences, 295(1078), 473–496.

Read the paper.

Why I’m Reading It

This paper is especially relevant to my butterfly wing project because it is one of the earliest mathematical attempts to explain wing pattern formation through diffusion and gene activation rather than simply describing the observed patterns.

It also serves as an important bridge between Turing’s abstract reaction–diffusion theory and biologically motivated models of butterfly development.

Overview

Murray proposes a mathematical mechanism capable of generating common butterfly wing patterns using a diffusing morphogen coupled with a threshold gene-activation process. He then compares the predictions of the model with experimental results from butterfly and moth wings.

The second half of the paper extends similar mathematical ideas to mammalian coat patterns, arguing that geometry and scale may explain much of the diversity observed in nature.

Main Ideas

Pattern Formation Requires a Mechanism

  • The goal is to explain how biological patterns arise, not simply describe them.
  • Mathematical models should propose mechanisms that can generate observed patterns.
  • The focus is on developmental processes rather than static pigmentation.

Diffusing Morphogens

  • A morphogen is released from localized sources on the developing wing.
  • Diffusion produces a spatial concentration gradient.
  • Cells measure the local morphogen concentration.
  • Developmental responses depend on concentration thresholds.
  • Temporary chemical gradients produce lasting spatial patterns.

Gene Activation as a Switch

  • Morphogen concentration does not continuously determine pigment.
  • Cells permanently switch developmental states once a critical threshold is exceeded.
  • The developmental decision persists even after the morphogen disappears.
  • Threshold activation stabilizes the final wing pattern.

Geometry Matters

  • Wing geometry is an active component of pattern formation.
  • Changing wing size can alter the resulting pattern.
  • Changing wing shape changes morphogen distributions.
  • Vein arrangement influences diffusion pathways.
  • The same governing equations can generate different patterns solely because of geometry.

Questions

  • Can Murray’s continuous diffusion model can be reformulated on a graph using graph Laplacians?
  • How well has this model held up over the last forty years?
  • Which parts of the mechanism have experimental support today?
  • How would the model change if diffusion occurred on a graph instead of a continuous surface?
  • Could real wing vein networks be extracted automatically from photographs and used directly in simulations?
  • How do modern models incorporate gene regulatory networks in addition to morphogen diffusion?

Vocabulary

  • Morphogen — A signaling molecule whose concentration varies across space and provides positional cues to developing cells.

  • Reaction–diffusion — A mathematical model in which interacting chemicals react locally while diffusing through tissue, producing spatial patterns.

  • Determination stream — A moving developmental boundary that records where morphogen concentration first exceeds the activation threshold, leaving behind permanently determined cells.

  • Positional information — Information encoded by a cell’s location, often inferred from local morphogen concentration, that guides developmental fate.

  • Threshold activation — The process by which a developmental response occurs only when morphogen concentration exceeds a critical value.

  • Gene switch — A regulatory mechanism in which cells permanently change developmental state once a threshold is crossed.

  • Diffusion field — The spatial distribution of morphogen concentration over the developing wing at a given time.

  • Geometry — The shape, size, and internal structure of the wing that influence how morphogens spread.

  • Scale — The physical dimensions of the tissue, which affect diffusion distances and therefore the resulting pattern.

  • Boundary conditions — Mathematical constraints imposed at the edges of the wing that determine how morphogens behave at tissue boundaries.
