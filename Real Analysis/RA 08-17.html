Major Themes in this Course

Convergence

A sequence \((a_n)\) converges to a limit \(L\) if its terms become arbitrarily close to \(L\) as \(n\) becomes sufficiently large.

Formally,

\[ a_n \to L \quad \text{if and only if} \quad \forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \exists N \in \mathbb{N} \text{ such that } n \geq N \implies |a_n-L|<\varepsilon. \]

In other words, no matter how small a tolerance \(\varepsilon\) we choose, all sufficiently late terms of the sequence lie within that tolerance of \(L\).

Convergence means that a sequence settles toward a particular number. The terms do not need to ever actually equal the limit; they only need to get as close to it as we demand.

For example,

\[ a_n = \frac{1}{n} \]

gives the sequence

\[ 1,\ \frac12,\ \frac13,\ \frac14,\ldots \]

whose terms approach \(0\). Thus

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}=0. \]

The formal definition turns the informal phrase “gets closer and closer” into a precise statement. We choose how close we want the terms to be using \(\varepsilon\), and then show that after some index \(N\), every remaining term stays that close.

Continuity

A function \(f\) is continuous at a point \(a\) if values of \(f(x)\) can be made arbitrarily close to \(f(a)\) by taking \(x\) sufficiently close to \(a\). Formally,

\[ \forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \exists \delta > 0 \text{ such that } |x-a|<\delta \implies |f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon. \]

Equivalently, \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) when

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a). \]

Informally, continuity means that small changes in the input produce small changes in the output. A continuous function has no sudden jump, hole, or break at the point under consideration.

In calculus, this is sometimes described by saying that we can draw the graph near the point “without lifting the pencil.” Real analysis replaces that visual intuition with a precise relationship between input error and output error.

Here,

\[ \delta \]

controls how close \(x\) must be to \(a\), while

\[ \varepsilon \]

controls how close \(f(x)\) must be to \(f(a)\).

Thus the definition can be read as

\[ \text{small change in input} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{small change in output}. \]

Differentiation

A function \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) if the limit

\[ f'(a) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \]

exists and is finite.

The derivative \(f'(a)\) describes the instantaneous rate of change of \(f\) at \(a\), or equivalently the slope of the tangent line to the graph of \(f\) at that point.

A derivative measures how quickly a quantity is changing at a particular instant.

Between two different points, we can calculate an ordinary average rate of change:

\[ \frac{\text{change in output}} {\text{change in input}} = \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}. \]

Geometrically, this is the slope of a secant line through two points on the graph.

To obtain the slope at exactly one point, we let the distance \(h\) between those points approach zero:

\[ h\to0. \]

If the resulting slopes approach a single finite number, that number is the derivative.

For example, if position is given by \(s(t)\), then

\[ s'(t) \]

represents instantaneous velocity. Thus differentiation converts information about a quantity into information about how that quantity is changing.

Integration

For a bounded function \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\), integration can be understood through increasingly fine partitions of the interval. If the corresponding Riemann sums converge to a common value \(I\) as the partition becomes arbitrarily fine, then \(f\) is Riemann integrable and

\[ I=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \]

The integral measures accumulated quantity and, geometrically, can be interpreted as signed area under the graph of \(f\).

Integration adds together infinitely many very small contributions to determine a total quantity.

The basic idea is to divide an interval into small pieces and approximate the area under a function using rectangles:

\[ \text{area of one rectangle} \approx f(x_i)\Delta x. \]

Adding the rectangles gives a Riemann sum,

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\Delta x. \]

As the partition becomes finer, the rectangles become narrower. If these approximations approach a single value, that value is the definite integral:

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx. \]

Integration is therefore fundamentally a process of approximation followed by a limit.

How the Ideas Fit Together

The central unifying idea is the limit. Many concepts from calculus can be reconstructed as different applications of convergence:

\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{Convergence} \\[4pt] \downarrow \\[4pt] \text{Limits} \end{array}} \]

Limits are then used to make the other major ideas precise:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Continuity:}\qquad &\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a), \\[6pt] \text{Differentiation:}\qquad &f'(a) =\lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}, \\[6pt] \text{Integration:}\qquad &\int_a^b f(x)\,dx =\lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\Delta x. \end{aligned} \]

In calculus, we learn how to compute many of these objects. In real analysis, the emphasis shifts toward understanding precisely why these definitions work, when the relevant limits exist, and what assumptions are required for the familiar theorems of calculus to be valid.

Differential Equations and Approximation

Differential equations describe how physical systems evolve.

Approximation often involves discretization. Important questions include:

  • Does the discretization scheme converge?
  • How fast does it converge?

Discretization introduces error. We can minimize this error by using sufficiently high resolution, or at least a resolution that reasonably matches the system being modeled.

The grid should be fine enough to capture the relevant behavior, but not so fine that computation is unnecessarily expensive.

Messy Cases

  • Riemann Rearrangement Theorem: for example, the alternating harmonic series.

Proof Hygiene

We handle messy cases with careful definitions and proofs.

A proof is the report of a successful search, not the transcript of one.

Precision vs. Intuition

A good proof should:

  • be precise in scope — neither broader nor narrower than the claim requires;
  • be logically complete and independently verifiable;
  • contain no unnecessary steps, cases, or assumptions;
  • make the underlying mechanism of the argument clear and generalizable;
  • omit failed approaches and irrelevant intermediate reasoning;
  • ensure that every statement contributes directly to the argument.

Correct \(\neq\) done.

Example Proof: \(\sqrt{3}\) is Irrational

Claim. \(\sqrt{3}\) is irrational.

Proof. Suppose, for contradiction, that \(\sqrt{3}\) is rational. Then we may write

\[ \sqrt{3} = \frac{p}{q}, \]

where \(p,q \in \mathbb{Z}\), \(q \neq 0\), and

\[ \gcd(p,q)=1. \]

Squaring both sides gives

\[ 3 = \frac{p^2}{q^2}, \]

so

\[ 3q^2 = p^2. \]

Thus \(3 \mid p^2\). Since \(3\) is prime, it follows that \(3 \mid p\). Therefore, for some \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\),

\[ p=3n. \]

Substituting this into \(3q^2=p^2\) gives

\[ 3q^2 = 9n^2, \]

and hence

\[ q^2 = 3n^2. \]

Therefore \(3 \mid q^2\), so \(3 \mid q\).

Thus \(3\) divides both \(p\) and \(q\), contradicting the assumption that

\[ \gcd(p,q)=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{\sqrt{3}\notin\mathbb{Q}}. \]
